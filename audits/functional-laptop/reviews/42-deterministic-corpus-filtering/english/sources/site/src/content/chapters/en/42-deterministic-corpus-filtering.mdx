## The problem: verified text is not yet training input

A matching checksum tells us which text we have, not whether every record should
enter a training corpus. A record may be malformed, too large for the processing
budget, or contain a literal marker that our chosen policy excludes. Some records
need review rather than an automatic yes or no.

Deleting every excluded record without keeping its reason loses useful evidence.
Reporting only the final retained count also hides which records each rule
actually examined. If an early rule stops a record, later rules cannot truthfully
report that the record passed or failed them.

The solution here is an ordered filter with three dispositions:

- **Retained:** a candidate for the next data-processing stage, not a guarantee
  of quality, privacy or permission.
- **Rejected:** excluded by an automatic rule.
- **Manual review:** withheld from downstream training input. This example has
  no approval override.

The first rule in the configured order that rejects a record or holds it for
manual review supplies that record's first terminal decision. Evaluation stops
there; later rules are not evaluated for that record. If a record survives every
rule, it is retained with no first terminal rule. The report preserves each
record's disposition, the rules actually evaluated, any first terminal rule and
the record's reference to its raw source. It does not copy excluded text into
public findings.

## Eight records, with explicit boundaries

Everything in the worked example is course-authored synthetic data. It is not a
filtering result for TinyStories. The two source names `fixture-train` and
`fixture-validation` preserve their raw-source provenance; they do not create
the final training, validation or test split.

The selected framing policy, `end-of-input-delimited-v2`, recognizes the ASCII
marker `<|endoftext|>` only when it occupies an entire physical line. A marker
line may end with LF, CRLF or end of input. Its bytes are a delimiter, not payload
text. The same marker inside an ordinary text line is not a delimiter.

End of input also closes a remaining nonempty payload, even without a final
newline. End of input immediately after a separator creates no extra record.
Consecutive separators create an empty payload that the filter can reject.
A failed read is an error, not an end-of-input boundary. These rules are selected
explicitly; the reader does not guess another format after a failure.

For this fixture, append `b"<|endoftext|>\n"` after every record below, in table
order within each source. Every separator therefore contributes $14$ bytes.
In Rust literals, `\n` denotes one LF byte and `\xff` one byte that is invalid
as standalone UTF-8.

| Label | Source | Complete raw payload construction | Payload bytes | First terminal decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `r0` | `fixture-train` | `b"cat sat.\n"` | $9$ | None; retained |
| `r1` | `fixture-train` | `b"\xff\n"` | $2$ | Strict UTF-8; rejected |
| `r2` | `fixture-train` | $65$ copies of `b'x'`, then `b"\n"` | $66$ | Raw size; rejected |
| `r3` | `fixture-train` | `b"hi\n"` | $3$ | Minimum normalized length; rejected |
| `r4` | `fixture-validation` | `b"....\n"` | $5$ | Minimum ASCII letters; rejected |
| `r5` | `fixture-validation` | `b"demo-key=CANARY\n"` | $16$ | Secret marker; rejected |
| `r6` | `fixture-validation` | `b"contact=alex@example.invalid\n"` | $29$ | Manual marker; held |
| `r7` | `fixture-validation` | `b"demo-key=CANARY contact=alex@example.invalid\n"` | $45$ | Secret marker; rejected |

The marker text and address are fictional inputs. Their presence in this table
explains the lesson; it is not permission to include captured secrets or personal
data in a real findings report.

## The solution: stop once, count at every reached rule

The fixture deliberately uses small thresholds so each failure is visible:

1. Reject a raw payload above $64$ bytes before decoding it.
2. Reject invalid UTF-8.
3. Normalize the decoded text and reject fewer than $4$ normalized UTF-8 bytes.
4. Reject fewer than $1$ ASCII letter.
5. Reject the literal `demo-key=CANARY`, matched without ASCII case distinctions.
6. Hold the literal `contact=`, matched case-sensitively.
7. Hold text below the configured ASCII-letter share.

Normalization replaces CRLF pairs with LF, then trims only space, tab, CR and LF
at the two edges. It preserves case, internal spaces and other Unicode
characters. Length after normalization is measured in UTF-8 **bytes**, not
characters or tokens.

For the last rule, count Unicode scalar values other than the six ASCII
whitespace characters: space, tab, LF, vertical tab, form feed and CR. The
numerator counts ASCII letters among those values. Compare a configured
fraction using checked integer cross-products, rather than rounding a
floating-point ratio. A zero denominator is unavailable and sends a reached
record to review; it is not an observed share of zero. The fixture's threshold
is $0/1$, so its last survivor passes this rule.

These thresholds are not the full-corpus policy. The planned corpus policy uses
a $65{,}536$-byte raw limit, at least $16$ normalized bytes, at least $8$ ASCII
letters and an ASCII-letter share of at least $1/2$, with its own selected marker
lists. For example, the fixture's short secret record would reach a different
first terminal rule under a stricter length threshold. None of the fixture's
rates are measurements of a real corpus.

For a named rule $k$, report its terminal fraction using the population that
actually reached it:

$$
r_k=\frac{n_{\mathrm{disposition},k}}{n_{\mathrm{seen},k}}
$$

## Symbols in the filtering fraction

- $k$ identifies a rule in the configured order.
- $n_{\mathrm{seen},k}$ counts records surviving every earlier rule and reaching
  rule $k$.
- $n_{\mathrm{disposition},k}$ counts reached records whose first terminal
  decision is rejection or a hold for review at rule $k$.
- $r_k$ is dimensionless. When $n_{\mathrm{seen},k}=0$, the report stores
  unavailable, not zero.

Four records reach the secret-marker rule, and it rejects two. Its terminal
fraction is $2/4=1/2$. Dividing by the original eight records gives $2/8=1/4$,
a different quantity: that rule's terminal decisions as a fraction of all input
records. Do not label the second quantity as the reached-population rate.

The reached populations are $8,7,6,5,4,2,1$, leaving one retained record.
The final dispositions must conserve the input count:

$$
8=1_{\mathrm{retained}}+6_{\mathrm{rejected}}+1_{\mathrm{manual\ review}}
$$

A binary exclusion summary gives $7$ ineligible records, but those are
$6$ rejections plus $1$ pending review. Calling all seven “rejected” discards
the distinction needed to explain their outcomes.

## Rust owns the framing and decisions

The framer accepts an ordinary `Read` source. The output interface separates
retained rows from body-free exclusion findings, without requiring a directory,
filename, HTTP source or storage backend. Supporting libraries provide JSON
encoding and SHA-256; the course code owns boundaries, rule order, terminal
decisions and accounting.

A bounded buffer retains only payloads within the selected size limit. An
oversized record is drained to its boundary and counted without retaining its
whole body. Read-buffer boundaries do not split records or invalidate a Unicode
character: UTF-8 decoding happens on the complete bounded payload.

A quoted marker can cause a false positive. Sensitive text without a configured
literal can pass. Likewise, few ASCII letters do not prove that a record is bad
or that it is not English, and many ASCII letters do not establish language or
quality. These heuristics need corpus-specific inspection, including inspection
of excluded text under appropriate access controls.

## Preserve counts without exposing excluded bodies

<RustSource
  path="rust/crates/llm-from-scratch/src/data/governance.rs"
  region="stage-accounting"
  caption="Increment a rule's seen count only when the record reaches it, then count survival or its first terminal decision."
  label="Rust reached-population accounting"
/>

A finding contains a source reference, half-open raw byte span, occurrence ID,
evaluated rule IDs and the terminal decision. It contains no excluded body,
snippet or matched capture. The occurrence ID binds the selected raw artifact,
source and payload IDs, raw file digest and span. Equal text at different spans
has different occurrence IDs. A changed filter policy does not change those raw
occurrence IDs, but does change the derived artifact's policy identity.

Per source and terminal reason, the report includes the first $32$ occurrence
IDs in input order as samples. The full finding stream and counters remain
complete: the sample bound is not permission to stop counting. Findings and
the receipt share a strict metadata budget below $100$ MiB. Exceeding that
budget fails the attempt; it does not silently truncate evidence.

Byte accounting uses separate bases. Separator bytes are outside raw payload
spans. Payloads rejected before decoding still contribute to the raw total.
Normalized byte totals include only records actually decoded and normalized.
A complete run reads $287$ physical bytes: $175$ payload bytes and $112$
delimiter bytes. Of the payload bytes, $68$ belong to records rejected before
decoding and $107$ are decoded. Normalization leaves $101$ bytes across decoded
records: $8$ retained, $65$ rejected and $28$ pending review. These are byte
counts, not token counts; excluded text still contributes to its applicable
accounting totals.

A retained row is checked again against the selected filtered artifact, policy,
source references, span and text digest before consumption. Relabeling a
manual-review payload or selecting an untrusted candidate's own manifest does
not make it eligible.

## History: inspect what filtering removes

A binary keep/drop report hides the reason for exclusion and the population that
each later rule actually inspected.

The C4 study examines text removed by filters; RefinedWeb later combines
filtering and deduplication in a web-scale corpus.

Dodge and colleagues inspect C4 exclusions and find disproportionate removal of
content concerning minority groups by its word blocklist. Their 2021
[Documenting Large Webtext Corpora: A Case Study on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.98/)
motivates examining removals rather than assuming that a filter is neutral.

Penedo and colleagues describe a large web corpus built with filtering and
deduplication, and evaluate language models trained on its data. Their 2023
[RefinedWeb paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/fa3ed726cc5073b9c31e3e49a807789c-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html)
concerns a much broader pipeline than this eight-record fixture. Its model
results do not certify our heuristics or isolate a benefit from one rule.

The Rust contrast below is course-local: it reduces our existing decisions to
one exclusion bit. It is not an implementation reproduced from either paper.
It counts seven excluded records but loses their rejection-versus-review
distinction and cannot say which later rule saw each one.

### Source withdrawal is a known-lineage calculation

Filtering also preserves source lineage so a later withdrawal can identify
recorded downstream artifacts. The separate fictional graph uses `r0` as a
**raw-source node alias**, not as the worked table's record label or an actual
occurrence digest. Its descendants are `a0`, `f0`, `s0`, `tok0` and `w0`.
Two paths reach `w0`; the result lists it once. The unrelated branch `f9`
belongs to a different source and is not in this withdrawal's descendant set.

## What continues toward the decoder

Only retained candidates continue to deduplication, final split construction and
tokenization. The candidate set is kept in deterministic source and span order.
Pending-review text cannot join it through a relabeled role or an incomplete
output.

A complete receipt preserves the selected policy, source references and counts.
That makes the filtering decision reproducible, not universally correct. The
scalar decoder remains unchanged, and this small example establishes neither
real-corpus retention rates nor trained-model quality, privacy or memorization
safety.
