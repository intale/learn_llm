## The problem: the model learns from the text we let through

A language model learns to predict tokens from the documents supplied during
training. Its optimizer does not decide whether those documents are appropriate
for the task, whether we may use them, or whether a purportedly unseen test
example also appears in the training input. Those decisions must happen before
training.

Repeated text creates another problem. If one training pass visits every stored
document occurrence, each copy of a document supplies another copy of its token
targets. The repeated text receives more influence without adding new examples.
Removing duplicates changes that weighting; it does not merely save disk space.

Corpus preparation therefore has two outputs: selected text and evidence about
how it was selected. We need source context, declared exclusion rules, duplicate
handling and protected evaluation boundaries. A large file or a successful
download supplies none of those conclusions on its own.

We will use [NVIDIA NeMo Curator](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/curator/v26.07/curate-text)
to prepare text. It is an external tool, not
part of the Rust language model, and another preparation tool can replace it if
it produces the same documented input for the model. Rust will only load the
prepared documents. The extended tokenizer in Chapter 3 will then learn from the
training documents, not the validation or test documents.

## The solution: prepare documents, then load a stable format

Keep one document as one record throughout the preparation pipeline. Retain a
stable document ID and the source information needed to trace that record. A
source record should identify where the text came from, its revision and the
applicable license and attribution. A checksum identifies selected bytes; it
does not establish permission to use those bytes or tell us what they contain.

Apply declared quality rules, inspect what those rules exclude, and handle
duplicates before final training input is frozen. Quality rules are proxies:
for example, a minimum word count rejects short records, not every unsuitable
record. A rule can also remove useful text. Inspect both survivors and exclusions
rather than interpreting a low exclusion rate as success.

Keep privacy review separate from quality scoring. Passing a word-count filter
or removing duplicates does not remove names, addresses, credentials or other
sensitive content. Withhold unresolved records from training; do not copy their
sensitive text into public reports. Retain enough source and transformation
information to identify affected derived corpora if an input is withdrawn.
Removing a source from a later corpus does not erase what an already trained
model may have learned from it.

### A six-document example

The practical example uses course-authored text, not a result for a large
training corpus. Its JSONL input contains these six documents:

| Document ID | Text | Source group | Relevant observation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `story-a` | A cat naps. | `family-a` | Three words |
| `story-b` | A cat naps. | `family-a` | An identical copy of `story-a` |
| `story-c` | A dog runs. | `family-b` | Three words |
| `story-d` | Rain feeds trees. | `family-c` | Three words |
| `story-e` | hi | `family-e` | One word |
| `story-f` | !!! | `family-f` | Punctuation only |

Configure NeMo's word-count filter to retain records with at least $3$ and at
most $20$ words. Its English setting counts whitespace-separated items; that
proxy is not a linguistic definition of a word. The $4$ story occurrences
survive; `hi` and the punctuation-only
record do not. Exact duplicate removal then leaves $3$ distinct story texts.
The punctuation-only record has one whitespace-separated item too; this
particular rule rejects it for length, not because it recognizes punctuation
as unsuitable language-model training text.
The duplicate-removal workflow may retain either cat-story ID; the result we
care about here is one cat-story occurrence, not a promised representative ID
or output-file order.

Before deduplication, the cat story occupies $2$ of the $4$ surviving document
occurrences. After deduplication, it occupies $1$ of $3$ distinct documents. Under
uniform sampling of these whole-stage document occurrences before separating
their roles, its selection probability would change from $2/4$ to $1/3$.
That calculation illustrates duplicate weighting, not a sampler used to train
on this fixture. Actual token-target weighting also depends on document length,
split selection and the batching policy; document fractions are not token
fractions.

NeMo's exact method groups hashes of text and identifies copies to remove.
It does not identify paraphrases, and hash grouping is not an adversarial
collision-proof comparison. NeMo's fuzzy method uses approximate similarity
search for near-duplicates. Its configuration trades missed matches against
unwanted matches; it does not certify a universal similarity cutoff or detect
all ways an evaluation example could have leaked into training.

### Freeze the roles of the prepared documents

The training split supplies examples for fitting the tokenizer and model.
The validation split supplies examples for selecting settings or deciding when
to stop training. The test split is reserved for final evaluation. Do not move
a difficult held-out document into training after seeing its score.

Related records must not be scattered across those roles. Treat an identified
duplicate family or other known related source group as one unit when assigning
splits. Otherwise two different IDs can put substantially the same example in
both training and evaluation. For this fixture, the two cat-story copies share
`family-a`; their different IDs do not make them independent examples.

For the exercise, a supplied policy assigns `family-a`, `family-e` and
`family-f` to training, `family-b` to validation and `family-c` to test. We fix
that policy before running the filters. Both cat-story copies therefore have
the same role regardless of which ID duplicate removal keeps. The prepared
fixture has one document in each role: the cat story in training, the dog story
in validation and the rain story in test.

The recipe uses Pandas to join those declared assignments to the source-group
metadata. It refuses an unassigned group rather than choosing a convenient role
after seeing the output. This checks the supplied groups, not whether we have
discovered every related pair. One held-out story is also far too little
evidence for a meaningful model-quality estimate.

Split disjointness and decontamination are different checks. Disjoint IDs do not
prove different text. Removing exact duplicates does not prove that near-copies
or external benchmark examples are absent. Declare the protected evaluation
material and the overlap method before inspecting results, report its measured
scope, and keep unresolved overlaps out of training. A check against a named
evaluation set does not establish internet-wide contamination freedom.

### Export documents, not preparation-specific objects

The handoff to Rust is UTF-8 **JSON Lines**: one JSON object per physical line.
Each object has a nonblank string `id` and a nonblank string `text`. Other fields
are retained as metadata. For example:

```json
{"id":"story-c","text":"A dog runs.","source_group":"family-b"}
```

The document ID is a label, not a token ID or a required filename. The text is
still text; tokenization comes next. A newline inside document text must be
escaped as `\n` inside the JSON string, so it does not create a second record.
Source and split records remain linked to the exported documents, but a JSONL
file alone does not certify that those decisions were made correctly.

## Formula: distinguish retention from quality

For a nonempty input corpus, the final document-retention fraction is

$$
r_{\mathrm{keep}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{prepared}}}{N_{\mathrm{input}}}
$$

{/* chapter-section:symbol-glossary */}

Here $N_{\mathrm{input}}$ is the number of input document occurrences,
$N_{\mathrm{prepared}}$ is the number remaining after the declared preparation
stages, and $r_{\mathrm{keep}}$ is a dimensionless fraction. Neither count is a
byte count or token count. With zero input documents, the fraction is undefined;
report that condition rather than inventing a zero-quality score.

In the six-document example, quality filtering retains $4/6$ of the original
input. Duplicate removal retains $3/4$ of its own input: the four records that
reached that stage. Their product is the final retention fraction,

$$
\frac{4}{6}\cdot\frac{3}{4}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}.
$$

Changing the denominator changes the question. Reporting $1/6$ as the duplicate
stage's exclusion fraction would use the original input population rather than
the $4$ records the duplicate stage examined. Keep every stage's entering and
leaving document counts so its fraction can be interpreted locally.

Keeping half the input says how much remains under this policy. It does not say
that half was bad, that the survivors are safe, or that a model trained on them
will perform well. Those claims need different evidence.

## Practical preparation with NeMo Curator

This workflow uses a separately built NeMo Curator image. You need Linux,
Docker, a compatible NVIDIA GPU and driver, and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit so
Docker can expose the GPU. The pinned NeMo release must be compatible with that
CUDA/driver stack. Rust's prepared-corpus reader does not require a GPU or CUDA.

Run the following commands from the repository root. The image build may use
network access to obtain the external tool. It does not acquire a training
corpus or add NeMo to the Rust/site image:

```sh
docker build --file docker/nemo-curator.Dockerfile \
  --tag learn-llm-nemo-curator:26.07 .
```

With the NeMo image built and the NVIDIA GPU available to Docker, run from the
repository root. Create a fresh output directory for the exercise, then run the
tool with network access disabled. The read-only mount supplies the recipe and
its six-record fixture; the writable mount receives generated preparation output:

```sh
mkdir -p .build/corpus/practical-ch02-exact-fixture
docker run --rm --network none --gpus all --memory=8g --cpus=2 --shm-size=1g \
  --hostname course-nemo --add-host course-nemo:127.0.0.1 \
  --mount type=bind,source="$PWD/tools/nemo-curator",target=/course,readonly \
  --mount type=bind,source="$PWD/.build/corpus/practical-ch02-exact-fixture",target=/output \
  learn-llm-nemo-curator:26.07 \
  /course/prepare_corpus.py \
    --input /course/fixtures/raw.jsonl --output /output \
    --split-policy /course/fixtures/splits.json
```

Use an empty output directory. If the directory already contains an earlier
attempt, choose a different directory rather than overwriting its evidence.
The hostname mapping lets the local Ray workers resolve their container on
loopback; it does not enable external network access.

The recipe invokes NeMo's reader, configured word-count filter, exact duplicate
identification and duplicate-removal workflow. Identification produces IDs to
remove; it is not already the prepared text. The removal workflow must consume
those IDs from the same input and ID policy, then export the remaining documents.

The filter configuration in `tools/nemo-curator/prepare_corpus.py` uses the
library's pipeline and stages; the recipe does not implement its own filter:

```python
Pipeline(
    name="course_corpus_quality",
    stages=[
        JsonlReader(file_paths=str(input_path), read_kwargs=read_kwargs),
        ScoreFilter(
            filter_obj=WordCountFilter(min_words=min_words, max_words=max_words, lang="en"),
            score_field="word_count",
        ),
        JsonlWriter(path=str(quality_path), mode="error"),
    ],
).run(executor=executor)
```

The surrounding recipe supplies the reader settings, starts a local Ray client,
and runs NeMo's exact-identification and text-removal workflows. The defaults
set `min_words` to `3` and `max_words` to `20`. Command-line options change those
values; retain them with the output report when comparing different policies.
The supplied split-policy file is independent of the observed scores.

Inspect the generated report and exported files with these commands, still from
the repository root:

```sh
cat .build/corpus/practical-ch02-exact-fixture/report.json
cat .build/corpus/practical-ch02-exact-fixture/prepared.jsonl
cat .build/corpus/practical-ch02-exact-fixture/train.jsonl
```

For this fixture, the expected
stage counts are $6$ input documents, $4$ after word-count filtering and $3$ after
exact duplicate removal. The final texts are `A cat naps.`, `A dog runs.` and
`Rain feeds trees.`; do not require one particular cat-story ID. The report also
records one document in each role. `prepared.jsonl` contains all three documents
for inspection; `train.jsonl`, `validation.jsonl` and `test.jsonl` contain their
respective selections and retain the `split` metadata.

This is a tiny execution example with a fixed assignment of known source
groups. It is not an approval of a larger corpus, a privacy check, proof that all
related groups were found or a trained-model result. The helper limits input to
$4$ MiB and $4096$ documents by default so the example is inspectable; those are
not a recipe for processing the previously acquired large corpus. A bulk run
needs its own feasible configuration and complete selection evidence.

For a real corpus, also retain input provenance, the tool version and configuration,
excluded-document counts, the duplicate policy, split assignments and the scope
of overlap and privacy review. A successful small fixture does not establish
that the same resource settings fit a larger dataset. Select and measure that
workload separately, preserving the raw inputs and every failed attempt.

## Load the prepared output in Rust

`PreparedCorpusReader` accepts any caller-supplied `BufRead` stream. A file,
memory buffer or database export can supply that stream without changing the
loader. Standard buffered I/O reads physical lines; Serde JSON parses their
objects. The loader preserves decoded text and metadata. It does not normalize
text, reject duplicate content or assign a split.

The caller chooses maximum source-record bytes, document count and cumulative
decoded-text bytes. Record bytes include the physical line ending; decoded text
bytes count the UTF-8 representation of the `text` value, not JSON syntax or
metadata. An oversized record, malformed JSON, missing required field, failed
read or exceeded limit stops the stream with an error. No failed document is
returned or counted. Documents returned earlier are not rolled back.

for its summary, and emits that summary only after the complete input succeeds.

not a transactional storage adapter: a caller storing documents as they arrive
must provide its own staging if partial results must stay unpublished.

## History: more text was not the whole solution

Collecting readable text alone does not document its intended use, control the
weight of repeated occurrences or protect held-out evaluation from training
overlap.

Dataset documentation makes source context reviewable; deduplication studies
connect repeated training text with memorization and train-test overlap in
their measured settings.

[Datasheets for Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010v8) was first posted in
$2018$. Gebru and colleagues propose documenting dataset motivation,
composition, collection and intended uses so those choices can be reviewed.
Documentation records context; it does not itself establish permission or
privacy.

The $2021$ [C4 analysis](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.98/) examined
where web-corpus text came from and what filtering removed. Its finding that
blocklist filtering disproportionately excluded text from and about minority
individuals is a reason to inspect exclusions, not to label every filter neutral.

In $2022$, [Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.577/)
studied duplicate handling in training corpora. Lee and colleagues report reduced memorized output and train-test
overlap after deduplication in their studied corpora and models; those outcomes
are not measurements of this course fixture. They explain why duplicate
handling and evaluation separation matter in addition to collecting and
documenting text.

The following Rust comparison loads two identical supplied records and keeps
both. It illustrates the limitation of merely reading raw occurrences, not a
reimplementation of those studies' preparation tools:

## Follow the preparation-to-learning boundary

The preparation tool owns the configured exclusions and duplicate handling.
The exported documents and their source and split evidence cross the boundary
into the Rust training pipeline. The Rust loader reads that selection; the
tokenizer learns only from the training role. Passing a record between stages
does not grant it a new quality or privacy guarantee.

## What reaches the language model next

Prepared training documents become the tokenizer's learning input in Chapter 3.
Held-out documents may be encoded with the learned tokenizer, but they must not
change its learned vocabulary or merge choices. Later batching creates token
targets from that same frozen training selection. NeMo has not implemented any
of those Rust LLM algorithms; it has supplied documented text for them to learn
from.
