## Control gradient magnitude and the rate of each update

A backward pass can produce a gradient dominated by a few large coordinates.
Feeding that gradient directly into the optimizer lets its magnitude determine
the optimizer's new moment values. Independently, a fixed learning rate gives
the same multiplier to the first and last updates. A training experiment may
instead call for a gradual increase at the start and smaller rates near the end.
These are two separate choices: control the gradient entering AdamW, and choose
the rate used by each successful update.

The existing trainer already performs global gradient-norm clipping and AdamW.
We will inspect that clipping operation and extend its existing learning-rate
schedule with linear warmup followed by one cosine decay. The optimizer, model
and update owner stay the same.

### One factor for gradients in two tensors

Start with a constructed scalar diagnostic on the text model. Its derivative
has values $3$ and $4$ in the first two coordinates of
`token_embedding.weight`, and $12$ in the first coordinate of
`blocks.0.attention_norm.gain`. Every other parameter-gradient coordinate is
zero. This is an inspection objective chosen to make its gradient easy to
check; it is not a next-token likelihood.

The complete gradient has Euclidean norm
$\sqrt{3^2+4^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=13$. Set the global norm limit to $6.5$.
Multiplying every coordinate by the same factor $6.5/13=0.5$ gives
$1.5$, $2$ and $6$, whose combined norm is $6.5$. The nonzero vector keeps
its direction. Clipping each tensor independently would change the relationship
between the two tensors' contributions.

The Rust diagnostic observes exactly those three effective gradient values,
applies one existing AdamW update at rate $0.001$, and clears gradients. It
uses its own fresh model and optimizer; the text-training experiment below
starts separately.

### Six successful updates, with two warmup updates

For the text experiment, plan six successful optimizer updates. Choose a peak
rate of $0.002$, a final decay rate of $0.0002$, and two warmup updates.
Linear warmup uses half the peak, $0.001$, on update $1$ and reaches
$0.002$ on update $2$. The four following updates move along one cosine
decay to the final rate.

The recorded rates for updates $3$, $4$ and $5$ are
$0.0017363961030678928$, $0.0011$ and $0.0004636038969321073$.
Update $6$ uses $0.0002$. These are the reported `f64` values, rather than
a claim that decimal arithmetic or every backend gives identical bits.
A successful update owns one rate; an attempted call that is refused or skipped
does not consume a rate.

## Define one global clip and one warmup–cosine schedule

Let $j$ enumerate scalar coordinates across all distinct named parameter
tensors, counting a tied parameter once. Let $g_j$ be a gradient coordinate
after any loss unscaling and before clipping. The complete gradient's Euclidean
norm is $r$, its finite positive limit is $c$, and $a$ is the one common
nonamplifying factor. AdamW receives effective coordinate $q_j$.

For the schedule, $t$ is the one-based successful optimizer-update index and
$T$ is the total planned number of those updates. $W$ counts warmup updates;
$P$ is the positive peak rate; $F$ is the positive final decay rate; and
$\eta_t$ is the rate assigned to update $t$. The constant $\pi$ is the usual
circle constant, and the cosine angles use radians. The ideal rules are

$$
\begin{aligned}
r&=\sqrt{\sum_j g_j^2},&
a&=\begin{cases}1,&r=0,\\ \min(1,c/r),&r>0,\end{cases}&
q_j&=a\,g_j,\\
\eta_t&=\begin{cases}
P\,t/W,&W>0,\ 1\le t\le W,\\
F+\dfrac{P-F}{2}\!\left[1+\cos\!\left(\pi\dfrac{t-W}{T-W}\right)\right],&W>0,\ W<t\le T,\\
F+\dfrac{P-F}{2}\!\left[1+\cos\!\left(\pi\dfrac{t-1}{T-1}\right)\right],&W=0,\ 1\le t\le T.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

The zero-norm branch avoids division by zero and leaves the gradient zero.
For a nonzero gradient, the factor is $1$ when the norm already fits and
$c/r$ when it exceeds the limit. Thus in exact arithmetic the effective
gradient has norm at most $c$, and a nonzero vector retains its direction.

With positive warmup, $t/W$ reaches $1$ on update $W$. The subsequent cosine
argument moves from $0$ toward $\pi$ as updates approach $T$, moving the
rate from $P$ to $F$. With zero warmup, update $1$ must already use $P$;
the cosine progress therefore starts from $t-1$ over $T-1$.
There is one decay and no restart. $F$ is the decay endpoint, not a lower
bound on rates earlier in warmup.

The warmup–cosine constructor requires $2\le T\le2^{53}$, $0\le W<T$ and finite rates
$0<F\le P$. The integer bound lets every index convert exactly to `f64`;
leaving $W<T$ reserves a decay update and a nonzero denominator. Allocation
must also succeed. The Rust code assigns peak and final endpoints explicitly
and passes every generated rate through the existing positive-rate validator.
Even positive ideal warmup values can underflow to stored zero and be refused.

The existing norm calculation rescales its sum of squares to avoid avoidable
overflow. Its gradient transform supports both uniform multiplication and a
normalized representation for numerical range. The equations describe the
ideal transformation; the implementation still performs represented `f64`
operations. When clipping occurs, the trainer's after-norm field records the
chosen limit. That field is not an independent remeasurement of every effective
coordinate. The three-coordinate diagnostic above supplies a separate hand
calculation of its resulting norm.

## Keep clipping, Adam moments and weight decay separate

Clipping limits the gradient supplied to AdamW's moments. It does not directly
limit the final parameter displacement, because AdamW also uses coordinatewise
moment normalization and a separate weight-decay term.

For one coordinate, write its effective gradient at update $t$ as $q_t$.
Let $m_t$ and $v_t$ be its first and second moments, with initial
$m_0=v_0=0$. Their coefficients $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ lie in $[0,1)$;
the bias-corrected moments are $\widehat m_t$ and $\widehat v_t$. Let $\epsilon>0$ be the
denominator offset, $\theta_{t-1}$ the old weight, and $\lambda$ its decay
coefficient. For a coordinate selected for decay, the existing AdamW rule is,
in exact arithmetic,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_t&=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)q_t,\\
v_t&=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)q_t^2,\\
\widehat m_t&=\frac{m_t}{1-\beta_1^t},&
\widehat v_t&=\frac{v_t}{1-\beta_2^t},\\
\theta_t&=\theta_{t-1}
-\eta_t\frac{\widehat m_t}{\sqrt{\widehat v_t}+\epsilon}
-\eta_t\lambda\theta_{t-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Schedule decay** lowers the learning rate $\eta_t$ across updates.
**Weight decay** subtracts $\eta_t\lambda\theta_{t-1}$ during an update,
outside the loss-gradient and moment calculation. Do not add that decay term
to the gradient before clipping. Both subtractions use the scheduled rate,
but they perform different operations.

The diagnostic and six-update text run use $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$,
$\epsilon=10^{-8}$ and $\lambda=0.01$. In the diagnostic's first coordinate,
the effective gradient is $1.5$. The stored first and second moments are
$0.14999999999999997$ and $0.1125000000000001$; the corrected moments are
$1.5$ and $2.25$. At rate $0.001$, the adaptive subtraction is
$0.0009999999933333335$ and the separate decay subtraction is
$9.270782062966737\times10^{-7}$. Starting from weight
$0.09270782062966737$, the stored result is $0.09170689355812774$.
These represented values expose the two branches; the cap on the input
gradient is not a cap on their combined parameter change.

## From recurrent-gradient control to scheduled transformer training

Exploding gradients made recurrent-network optimization difficult; controlling
their norm addresses gradient size rather than defining a complete training
schedule. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio propose gradient norm clipping for
exploding gradients in recurrent neural networks. Their paper,
[On the difficulty of training Recurrent Neural Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.5063v2),
was first submitted in 2012; the cited revision is from 2013. This supports
the norm-control idea, not a guarantee about vanishing gradients or AdamW
convergence.

Loshchilov and Hutter separate weight decay from the loss-gradient update in
adaptive optimization.
[Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101v3)
was first submitted in 2017; the cited revision is from 2019. That distinction
is why this lesson leaves decay outside the clipping and moment branch and
reuses the course's existing AdamW implementation.

LLaMA combines AdamW with gradient clipping and a learning-rate schedule for
transformer language-model training. Touvron and colleagues' 2023 LLaMA report
uses AdamW, a cosine schedule ending at one tenth of the maximum rate, two
thousand warmup steps and gradient clipping with threshold one. See its
[optimizer section](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.13971v1#S2.SS3).
Our six-update experiment and two warmup updates are a small teaching choice.
Its text-run norm limit $0.5$ and decay coefficient $0.01$ also belong to this
experiment; this run does not reproduce LLaMA training.

In this decoder, clipping controls the gradient entering AdamW, while the
schedule chooses the rate associated with each successful optimizer update.
Reuse the existing global clipping and AdamW update, adding linear warmup
followed by one cosine decay over successful optimizer updates. The Rust
example makes norm control, scheduled rates and preserved state inspectable
on tiny text-model fixtures; it does not reproduce the papers' training runs
or establish comparative language-model quality.

### Advance the clock only when AdamW commits

The requested update must equal the optimizer's committed count plus one.
This check runs before the forward callback. Looking up a rate does not
consume it.

<RustSource path="rust/crates/llm-from-scratch-practical/src/training/trainer.rs"
  region="scheduled-update-clock" captionId="optimizer-schedule-rust-clock"
  caption="Require the requested learning-rate entry to match the next optimizer update before running forward."

The text run reuses Chapter 11's seed-$39$ model fixture: vocabulary $258$,
residual width $4$, one attention head, feed-forward width $8$, two decoder
blocks and capacity for $8$ positions. It has $20$ named parameter tensors
and $1372$ learned scalars. Input IDs `[0,99,100]` have shape $[1,3]$, meaning
one row and three positions; targets `[99,100,1]` provide three next-token
targets for the mean negative log likelihood. The same fixed text is reused
at every update.

Within this run, one live model and one AdamW instance continue through all
six generated rates. Computation is CPU `f64`, dropout is disabled and the
loss scale is $1$. The unscaled entry and the scaled/accumulated entries share
the same finish owner. After contributions combine, its logical operations are
unscaling when applicable, checking the finite global gradient, clipping once,
applying scheduled AdamW once and clearing gradients. The gradient transform
can fuse unscaling and clipping before the moment calculation. Microbatches and
recomputed forwards do not create extra schedule steps.

After committing update $1$, the run deliberately requests update $3$.
It receives `OptimizerStepMismatch` with
`"optimizer committed step 1, expected 3"`, and `forward_called` is `false`.
The error's expected index comes from the requested $3$; the next eligible
update is still $1+1=2$. Weight and gradient bits, parameter revisions,
populated moments, beta powers and optimizer step remain unchanged.
The default schedule still supplies $0.002$ for update $2$.
The run then actually executes update $2$ and continues through $6$.
Executing the next valid update is not retrying the refused request for $3$.

### Inspect actual same-step retries in a separate fixed-rate fixture

The demonstration also calls Chapter 12's existing public `overflow_fixture`.
That experiment creates another model and optimizer, retains the fixed rates
`[0.001,0.001]`, and uses the existing CPU FP16/F32 matrix policy and its
original settings, including $\beta_2=0.999$. These rates are separate from
the generated cosine schedule.

After successful update $1$ populates moments, scheduled update $2$ overflows
and is skipped eight times. The first skip lowers the loss scale from
$16777216$ to $8388608$ and preserves weight and gradient bits, parameter
revisions, AdamW moments, beta powers and optimizer step $1$. The fixture
actually retries that same scheduled update $2$; it succeeds at scale
$65536$, commits optimizer step $2$ and clears gradients.

A numerical skip performs no AdamW update or decoupled weight decay and consumes
no learning-rate step. Its loss-scale run state can change. This state boundary
does not promise rollback of arbitrary caller effects, a data cursor or live
random-number state. Applying AdamW to zero gradients would still be an optimizer
step and could decay weights; an overflow skip does neither.

## Preserve the update clock before preserving model state

The text encoder, source-independent transformer, tied head and existing AdamW
now share generated rates indexed by committed logical updates. Global clipping
still transforms the complete gradient before moments; refused and
overflow-skipped attempts consume no optimizer step. The backbone continues to
consume vectors, positions and attention layout without requiring a tokenizer
or vocabulary. Text remains the worked input and output.

Chapter 15 will address portable model saving and loading in a fresh process,
followed by the additional state needed for training continuation. Saving the
learned composition, weights, settings and required assets for inference is a
different responsibility from retaining optimizer, random-generator and data
position state for exact continuation.

Full-corpus training, physical-GPU measurements and accelerated mixed-precision
execution still need their own evidence. The reused CPU FP16/F32 fixture is a
precision experiment, not a physical-GPU result.
