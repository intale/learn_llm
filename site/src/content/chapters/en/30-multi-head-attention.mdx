## Predict the boundaries before calculating probabilities

Use one batch with three token rows, model width $d_{\mathrm{model}}=4$, and
head count $h=2$. The per-head width is

$$
d_h=\frac{d_{\mathrm{model}}}{h}=2.
$$

This chapter applies RoPE to every complete head, so $d_h$ must also be even.
That is why $h=4$ is rejected here even though it divides a model width of $4$:
it would leave one unpaired coordinate per head.

The frozen input is

$$
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&1&0\\
\cos(1)&-\sin(1)&0&1\\
\cos(2)&-\sin(2)&1&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For this fixture only, $W_Q=W_K=W_V=I$. That makes the projected head blocks
easy to inspect. It is not a restriction of multi-head attention: in a trained
layer, the columns assigned to any head can read every input feature.

Before looking at the Rust output, predict this shape sequence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X:[1,3,4]
&\to(Q,K,V):[1,3,4]
\to\operatorname{split}(Q,K,V):[1,2,3,2],\\
\operatorname{split}(Q,K,V):[1,2,3,2]
&\to A:[1,2,3,3]
\to H:[1,2,3,2],\\
H:[1,2,3,2]
&\to\operatorname{Concat}(H):[1,3,4]
\xrightarrow{W_O}\operatorname{MHA}(X):[1,3,4].
\end{aligned}
$$

The first two input coordinates deliberately counter-rotate position. At token
position $t$, they equal $R(-t)[1,0]$, so Chapter 29's rotation returns

$$
R(t)R(-t)[1,0]=[1,0].
$$

Head $0$ therefore compares three identical query/key rows. Predict its causal
probabilities before running anything:

$$
A^{(0)}=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&0\\
\tfrac12&\tfrac12&0\\
\tfrac13&\tfrac13&\tfrac13
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Head $1$ receives $[1,0]$, $[0,1]$, and $[1,1]$ before rotation, so its allowed
scores are not equal. You can still predict that both heads have exactly zero
probability above the diagonal.

Now carry token position $1$ through both heads. Head $0$ averages its first two
unrotated value rows, not its rotated query rows:

$$
H^{(0)}_1
=\tfrac12[1,0]+\tfrac12[0.540302,-0.841471]
=[0.770151,-0.420735].
$$

For head $1$, the two visible scaled scores and their softmax are

$$
[s^{(1)}_{1,0},s^{(1)}_{1,1}]
=[-0.595010,0.707107]
\xrightarrow{\operatorname{softmax}}
[0.213809,0.786191].
$$

Its unrotated value rows are $[1,0]$ and $[0,1]$, so
$H^{(1)}_1=[0.213809,0.786191]$. Concatenating the two head rows gives
$[0.770151,-0.420735,0.213809,0.786191]$.

Finally, predict where features from the two heads can first influence the same
learned output coordinate. Splitting and both attention operations keep the
lanes separate; concatenation merely places their coordinates side by side.
After the separate attention calculations, only multiplication by $W_O$ permits
learned cross-head mixing. The frozen $W_O$ swaps the blocks, which demonstrates
learned routing; exercise 5 adds an off-block entry to demonstrate an actual sum
across heads. The earlier dense query/key/value projections may already use
every coordinate of the shared input row.

## Project into head views before scoring

The implementation stores three packed, bias-free matrices:

$$
Q=XW_Q,\qquad K=XW_K,\qquad V=XW_V,
$$

with $Q$, $K$, and $V$ shaped $[B,T,d_{\mathrm{model}}]$. A reshape and
transpose then produce $[B,h,T,d_h]$.

The packed form is just an efficient organization of the paper's per-head
columns. For queries,

$$
W_Q=[W_1^Q\;W_2^Q\;\cdots\;W_h^Q],
\qquad Q_i=XW_i^Q,
$$

and the same relationship holds for keys and values. This order matters:
splitting raw $X$ first would confine a head to a fixed input slice. Projecting
first lets every $W_i^Q$, $W_i^K$, and $W_i^V$ read the whole input row before
their outputs enter separate head lanes.

For head $i$, apply RoPE only to its query and key rows:

$$
\widetilde Q_i=\operatorname{RoPE}(Q_i),\qquad
\widetilde K_i=\operatorname{RoPE}(K_i).
$$

Then reuse the causal attention operation from Chapter 28:

$$
A_i=\operatorname{softmax}_{j}\!\left(
\frac{\widetilde Q_i\widetilde K_i^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}+M
\right),
\qquad
H_i=A_iV_i.
$$

$M$ blocks future keys, and the softmax runs separately over each head's key
positions. A single full-width attention calculation would produce one
normalized $[T,T]$ table shared by every value feature. Multi-head attention
instead produces $h$ separately normalized $[T,T]$ tables, which may differ
because their projected queries and keys may differ.

Each $A_i$ has shape $[B,T,T]$. Each $H_i$ has shape $[B,T,d_h]$. Restore the
model width by concatenating on the final feature axis, then apply one learned
output matrix:

$$
\operatorname{MHA}(X)=\operatorname{Concat}(H_1,\ldots,H_h)W_O
$$

where

$$
W_O\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}\times d_{\mathrm{model}}}.
$$

Concatenation has no parameters and performs no averaging. $W_O$ can preserve,
swap, add, or otherwise recombine coordinates produced by different heads. The
target implementation has four bias-free model-width matrices, so

$$
N_\theta=4d_{\mathrm{model}}^2.
$$

For $d_{\mathrm{model}}=4$, that is $64$ scalar parameters.

## Keep the model, head, and token axes distinct

- $\operatorname{MHA}$ is the complete multi-head causal self-attention layer.
- $X$ is its batch of input hidden-state rows.
- $\operatorname{Concat}$ joins the head outputs along their feature axis at
  each token position; it does not normalize or mix them.
- $B$ is the batch size.
- $T$ is the number of token positions in each sequence.
- $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ is the input and output width of the complete layer.
- $h$ is the number of independently normalized attention heads.
- $d_h=d_{\mathrm{model}}/h$ is the width of one head; it is even here because
  RoPE rotates adjacent coordinate pairs.
- $t$ is a zero-based token position in the worked example, and $R(t)$ is the
  two-dimensional rotation by $t$ radians used by its first head.
- $W_Q$, $W_K$, and $W_V$ are packed model-width projection matrices. The
  columns belonging to head $i$ form $W_i^Q$, $W_i^K$, and $W_i^V$.
- $Q_i$, $K_i$, and $V_i$ are head $i$'s projected query, key, and value rows.
- $\widetilde Q_i$ and $\widetilde K_i$ are those query and key rows after
  RoPE; values are not rotated.
- $M$ is the causal mask that blocks keys to the right of a query position.
- $j$ indexes key positions and is the axis normalized by each row softmax.
- $A_i$ is head $i$'s row-normalized causal attention table.
- $H_i=A_iV_i$ is head $i$'s weighted value mixture.
- $W_O$ is the learned output matrix applied after all $H_i$ rows have been
  concatenated.
- $N_\theta$ is the number of learned scalar parameters. $\delta$ is the
  central-difference perturbation, $\varepsilon_g$ is its accepted error, and
  $\varepsilon$ is the tolerance for the assembly and invariance checks.

The equations follow the paper-style convention $i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}$. Rust and
the frozen trace use zero-based head IDs $0,\ldots,h-1$, so trace heads $0$ and
$1$ correspond to $H_1$ and $H_2$ in this two-head example.

The head axis and token axis serve different roles. Each head gets its own
probability distribution over token positions, while concatenation joins head
features at the same token position. No probability normalization occurs across
heads.

## From one recurrent alignment to parallel projected attention

In their [neural machine translation paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473),
Bahdanau et al. replace one fixed source-sentence vector with a target-dependent
context vector, but the recurrent decoder still forms one alignment distribution
and context at a time rather than several parallel projected self-attention
heads. Each context is a weighted sum of encoder annotations whose scores depend
on the previous decoder state. This is an important learned-attention predecessor,
but it is not Transformer self-attention: the paper does not define queries,
keys, values, causal masks, or several parallel heads.

One scaled dot-product head, as built in Chapter 27, gives each query row one
learned comparison space, one probability distribution over key positions, and
one value mixture. In [*Attention Is All You Need*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762),
Vaswani et al. project queries, keys, and values several times, perform the
projected attention functions in parallel, concatenate their outputs, and
project the concatenation. The authors motivate this as an opportunity to
attend jointly to information from different representation subspaces and
positions.

That motivation is not a specialization guarantee. Heads share $X$, are trained
together, and are coupled again by $W_O$. A particular trained model may show
repeated, mixed, or difficult-to-interpret head behavior. This fixture proves
only that one valid parameter setting produces two different causal tables and
keeps their lanes separate through concatenation and until $W_O$.

[The LLaMA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971) provides a concrete modern causal
language-model example. Touvron et al. document a Transformer-based model with
multiple attention heads, RoPE at every layer, and an optimized causal
multi-head attention implementation. That optimized implementation avoids
storing attention weights. The dense calculation below keeps those weights
visible for inspection; it does not reproduce that optimized kernel.

The executable comparison does not pretend to reproduce an entire recurrent
decoder. It computes the defining weighted source-context sum for one fixed
alignment distribution, runs the existing single-head causal operation on the
same three-token input, and compares its one $[1,3,3]$ probability tensor with
the multi-head fixture's $[1,2,3,3]$ tensor. Both have normalized rows, but the
latter contains two independently normalized tables. The comparison does not
attribute the fixture's packed-matrix layout, even-width rule, bias policy,
names, or values to the papers:

## Keep the complete Rust path differentiable and inspectable

The public `split_heads` helper uses a reshape followed by a transpose:

$$
[B,T,d_{\mathrm{model}}]
\to[B,T,h,d_h]
\to[B,h,T,d_h].
$$

`merge_heads` applies the inverse transpose and reshape. Both operations remain
on the autodiff tape; there is no second hand-written copying algorithm.

Misconception: multi-head attention splits the raw input—or a completed
single-head result—into chunks.

Correction: learned projections run before the split. Each projected head then
computes its own rotated query/key scores, causal softmax, and value mixture.

specialization in every trained model.
