## Predict one pair before looking at the table

Use one two-coordinate query/key pair and the first frequency:

$$
q=[1,0],\qquad k=[0,1],\qquad \theta_0=1.
$$

At equal positions $m=n=0$, neither vector rotates, so

$$
q^\top k=0.
$$

Now put the query at $m=1$ while the key stays at $n=0$:

$$
R(1)q\approx[0.540302,0.841471],\qquad R(0)k=[0,1].
$$

Predict their dot product. It is approximately $0.841471$. Then shift both
absolute positions by $2$. The pair becomes $(m,n)=(3,2)$, but the difference
$n-m=-1$ is unchanged, so the dot product remains approximately $0.841471$.
Both rotated coordinates change; their relationship does not.

The executable fixture expands this idea to $d=4$. It repeats
$[1,0,1,0]$ at positions $0$, $1$, and $2$, and uses $b=100$. The two
frequencies are

$$
[\theta_0,\theta_1]=[1,0.1].
$$

Before running it, predict which pair turns faster, what happens at position
$0$, and which cells of the query-key grid should match after every position
is shifted by $3$.

## Rotate adjacent coordinates by absolute position

For each adjacent feature pair,

$$
\left(\operatorname{RoPE}(x_m)\right)_{2k:2k+2}=R(m\theta_k)(x_m)_{2k:2k+2}
$$

The rotation matrix and frequency schedule are

$$
R(\phi)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\theta_k=b^{-2k/d}.
$$

For this course, coordinates $2k$ and $2k+1$ form an adjacent pair. That
layout is an explicit implementation convention, not a claim that every RoPE
implementation stores pairs the same way. The feature width $d$ must be
nonzero and even so no coordinate is left without a partner.

Position $m=0$ gives a zero angle for every pair:

$$
R(0)=I.
$$

Each rotation is orthogonal, so it preserves the pair norm and therefore the
full vector norm:

$$
R(\phi)^\top R(\phi)=I,
\qquad
\lVert R(\phi)x\rVert_2=\lVert x\rVert_2.
$$

The important attention identity appears when a query at $m$ meets a key at
$n$:

$$
\bigl(R(m\theta_k)q_m^{(k)}\bigr)^\top
\bigl(R(n\theta_k)k_n^{(k)}\bigr)
=(q_m^{(k)})^\top R((n-m)\theta_k)k_n^{(k)}.
$$

It follows from

$$
R(a)^\top R(b)=R(b-a).
$$

The rotations receive absolute positions. The relative quantity $n-m$ appears
only after the two rotations combine in the dot product. The score still
depends on the query/key contents and on every $\theta_k$; it is not a function
of distance alone.

In reverse mode, the input adjoint uses the transposed rotation:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{x}_{2k}\\\bar{x}_{2k+1}\end{bmatrix}
=R(m\theta_k)^\top
\begin{bmatrix}\bar{y}_{2k}\\\bar{y}_{2k+1}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

## Keep position, pair, and visibility roles distinct

- $\operatorname{RoPE}$ is the pairwise rotary transformation applied
  independently to a query or key.
- $x_m$ is one query or key feature vector assigned to absolute position $m$.
- $m$ includes any caller-supplied sequence offset.
- $k$ selects one frequency and one adjacent feature pair.
- $2k:2k+2$ is the half-open slice containing coordinates $2k$ and $2k+1$.
- $R(\phi)$ is the stated counterclockwise two-dimensional rotation.
- $\theta_k$ is the angle advanced per position for pair $k$.
- $b$ is a positive finite frequency base; the fixture uses $b=100$ to keep
  both rates easy to inspect.
- $d$ is the nonzero even feature width.
- $n-m$ is the key position relative to the query position.

RoPE changes query-key score geometry. Chapter 28's causal mask still decides
whether a key is visible. Positive relative offsets remain present in the
unmasked grid below; they are not silently marked as allowed or blocked by
RoPE. Values $V$ are not rotated in this chapter.

## From recurrent order to rotary score geometry

This is the history of neural language-model structure on the road to modern
LLMs, not the history of Rust, another programming language, or a tensor
library.

[Vaswani et al., *Attention Is All You Need*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)
remove recurrent and convolutional layers from the Transformer. Without those
structural paths for order, they add position encodings to the input embeddings
at the bottoms of the encoder and decoder stacks. Their model uses several
sine/cosine frequencies; they also report a learned-position alternative with
nearly identical translation results in that comparison.

Vaswani et al. remove recurrence and convolution, add position encodings to
input embeddings, use multiple sinusoidal frequencies, and report a
learned-position alternative with nearly identical translation results in
their comparison.

Recurrent neural language models carried token order through a state updated
one step at a time; removing recurrence made order unavailable unless the
attention model received explicit position information.

The original paper says the sinusoidal choice was motivated by the hypothesis
that fixed relative offsets would be easy to learn. It does not prove that any
model will extrapolate to every longer context.

[Su et al., *RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864)
move position information into multiplicative rotations of query and key
subspaces. Their construction encodes absolute position while making a
relative position term explicit in the self-attention inner product.

Su et al. encode absolute position with rotations of query and key subspaces
and derive a self-attention inner product whose positional term uses the
difference between their positions.

The original Transformer added fixed sinusoidal or learned position vectors to
input embeddings. RoFormer instead rotates query and key feature pairs by
absolute position so their inner product exposes a relative offset.

[Touvron et al., *LLaMA: Open and Efficient Foundation Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971)
provide one bounded modern example. They document that the original LLaMA
removes absolute positional embeddings and applies RoPE at each layer.

Touvron et al. document that the original LLaMA removes absolute positional
embeddings and adds RoPE at each layer of its Transformer network.

Rotary position embeddings are used by influential decoder-only LLMs such as
the original LLaMA, which applies RoPE at every layer; they shape query-key
scores while causal masking remains a separate visibility rule.

That example does not make RoPE universal, guarantee length extrapolation, or
make a whole decoder invariant to shifting its input. The fixture proves a
local identity for fixed query/key vectors.

## Predict, then check the invariant

1. Prove that position $m=0$ is the identity for every pair.
2. Compute the single-pair result for $(m,n)=(1,0)$.
3. Predict what changes after adding the same offset to both $m$ and $n$.
4. Explain why shifting only $m$ changes the dot product.
5. Derive norm preservation from $R(\phi)^\top R(\phi)=I$.
6. Decide whether $V$ or the causal mask should be rotated.
7. Explain why a final feature width of $3$ is rejected by this full-width
   adjacent-pair implementation.
8. Distinguish the absolute positions passed to RoPE from the relative offset
   that appears in the dot product.
9. Explain why the common-shift identity for fixed vectors does not guarantee
   that a complete decoder prediction is shift-invariant.

<details>
  <summary>Check the key distinctions</summary>

  1. Position $m=0$ gives angle $0$ for every pair, so $R(0)=I$.
  2. For $(m,n)=(1,0)$, the rotated query dotted with the unchanged key is
     approximately $0.841471$.
  3. Adding the same offset $c$ keeps $(n+c)-(m+c)=n-m$, so the fixed-content
     dot product stays unchanged even though both absolute rotations change.
  4. Shifting only $m$ changes $n-m$ and therefore generally changes the
     query-key dot product.
  5. Since $R(\phi)^\top R(\phi)=I$, multiplying by the rotation preserves the
     squared norm and hence the norm.
  6. This chapter rotates $Q$ and $K$, not $V$; the causal mask separately
     decides which keys are visible.
  7. Width $3$ leaves one coordinate without a partner, so this full-width
     adjacent-pair implementation rejects it.
  8. Absolute indices enter each local rotation; the relative offset emerges
     only when the query and key rotations combine in their dot product.
  9. The common-shift identity holds for fixed query and key contents. Other
     decoder operations can still respond to absolute placement and changed
     context, so it is not a whole-model invariance guarantee.
</details>

Misconception: RoPE is relative because it receives relative indices.

Correction: it receives absolute positions. The shared rotation identity makes
the resulting fixed query-key dot product expose their relative difference.
RoPE does not replace causal masking, and a rotated score still depends on
content and frequency.

## Hand the position-aware axis to multiple heads

The cumulative decoder can now rotate each query and key row before the Chapter
28 causal score computation. Chapter 30 partitions that position-aware feature
axis into multiple heads, applies the same rotation and causal attention within
each head, concatenates head outputs, and applies the learned output projection
$W_O$.

Partial rotary dimensions, alternate pair layouts, key/value cache storage,
prefill, and long-context frequency scaling remain deliberately outside this
chapter.
